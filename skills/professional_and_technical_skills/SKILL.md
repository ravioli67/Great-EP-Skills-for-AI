# 10. Advanced Professional and Technical Skills

## 10.1 Ambulatory ECG Monitoring
- Explain the purpose of ambulatory electrocardiographic (ECG) monitoring.
- Differentiate Holter monitors, patch monitors, event recorders, mobile cardiac telemetry, and implantable loop recorders.
- Select monitoring approaches based on symptom frequency, suspected arrhythmia, and clinical objectives.
- Explain the importance of correlating symptoms with recorded cardiac rhythms.
- Recognize the limitations of short-duration monitoring for infrequent symptoms.
- Identify common recording artifacts and distinguish them from genuine arrhythmias.
- Assess signal quality, recording duration, and analyzable ECG time.
- Recognize atrial fibrillation, supraventricular tachycardia, ventricular arrhythmias, and bradyarrhythmias.
- Identify pauses, conduction abnormalities, and clinically significant ectopic activity.
- Interpret findings in relation to symptoms, medications, and underlying cardiac disease.
- Recognize urgent findings that require prompt clinical communication.
- Document monitoring results, limitations, and relevant clinical correlations.

## 10.2 Holter and Event Monitor Analysis
- Explain the differences between continuous Holter monitoring and event-triggered recording.
- Review average, minimum, and maximum heart rates.
- Assess underlying rhythm and heart rate variability when reported and clinically relevant.
- Identify premature atrial complexes and premature ventricular complexes.
- Evaluate the frequency, distribution, and patterns of ectopic beats.
- Recognize pauses, sinus bradycardia, and possible sinus node dysfunction.
- Identify first-degree, second-degree, and complete AV block when supported by the recording.
- Assess episodes of atrial fibrillation, flutter, and supraventricular tachycardia.
- Recognize nonsustained and sustained ventricular tachycardia.
- Review symptom markers and patient diaries alongside rhythm recordings.
- Differentiate artifact from arrhythmia using multiple ECG channels when available.
- Explain the limitations of automated rhythm classification.
- Verify clinically significant automated detections through manual review.
- Summarize arrhythmia burden, episode duration, and relevant symptom correlations.
- Escalate potentially dangerous findings according to clinical protocols.

## 10.3 Implantable Loop Recorder Evaluation
- Explain the purpose of implantable loop recorders (ILRs) in long-term rhythm monitoring.
- Identify common indications, including unexplained syncope and suspected intermittent arrhythmias.
- Explain automatic event detection and patient-triggered recording.
- Review stored electrograms and associated event metadata.
- Recognize atrial fibrillation, pauses, bradycardia, and tachycardia detections when supported by recordings.
- Assess the timing, duration, and recurrence of stored episodes.
- Identify false detections caused by noise, undersensing, oversensing, or signal artifact.
- Recognize that single-vector recordings may limit rhythm discrimination.
- Correlate stored episodes with symptoms and clinical history.
- Explain the importance of reviewing detection settings and device-specific algorithms.
- Recognize device alerts that require timely clinical assessment.
- Document findings and recommend appropriate specialist review.

## 10.4 Exercise-Related Arrhythmias
- Explain how exercise and sympathetic activation influence heart rate and cardiac electrophysiology.
- Recognize the expected physiological increase in heart rate during exertion.
- Identify exercise-associated premature beats and sustained or nonsustained tachyarrhythmias.
- Differentiate physiological sinus tachycardia from pathological arrhythmias.
- Assess arrhythmias occurring during exercise and recovery.
- Recognize exertional syncope or presyncope as potentially high-risk symptoms.
- Explain the role of exercise ECG testing in selected arrhythmia evaluations.
- Recognize exercise-induced ventricular ectopy that may warrant further assessment.
- Describe how ischemia, structural heart disease, and inherited arrhythmia syndromes may influence exercise-related risk.
- Understand the importance of test indications, contraindications, and appropriate monitoring.
- Recognize when exercise testing should be stopped according to established protocols.
- Correlate findings with symptoms, workload, blood pressure, and ECG changes.
- Identify clinical findings that require urgent assessment or specialist referral.

## 10.5 Inherited Arrhythmia Syndromes
- Explain the role of inherited abnormalities in cardiac ion channels and structural proteins.
- Differentiate channelopathies from inherited cardiomyopathies.
- Recognize the importance of personal and family history of unexplained syncope or sudden cardiac death.
- Explain the role of resting ECG, exercise testing, ambulatory monitoring, and specialist assessment.
- Describe the potential contribution of genetic testing and family screening.
- Recognize variable clinical expression and incomplete penetrance in inherited disorders.
- Explain why a normal resting ECG does not exclude every inherited arrhythmia syndrome.
- Understand the importance of specialist interpretation and genetic counseling where appropriate.

### 10.5.1 Long QT Syndrome
- Explain the relationship between prolonged ventricular repolarization and long QT syndrome (LQTS).
- Differentiate congenital LQTS from acquired QT prolongation.
- Identify QTc prolongation and relevant T-wave abnormalities on the ECG.
- Recognize torsades de pointes as a potentially life-threatening ventricular arrhythmia associated with prolonged repolarization.
- Assess relevant history, including exertional or emotion-associated syncope and unexplained seizures.
- Explain how electrolyte abnormalities and QT-prolonging medications may increase risk.
- Describe the role of clinical diagnostic criteria, family history, and genetic testing.
- Recognize the importance of individualized treatment, medication review, and activity counseling.
- Escalate suspected high-risk presentations for specialist evaluation.

### 10.5.2 Brugada Syndrome
- Explain the relationship between Brugada syndrome and ventricular arrhythmia risk.
- Recognize the characteristic type 1 ECG pattern involving the right precordial leads.
- Differentiate a diagnostic type 1 pattern from nondiagnostic or nonspecific ECG changes.
- Explain how fever and selected medications may unmask or aggravate the ECG pattern.
- Recognize the importance of excluding alternative causes of Brugada-like ECG findings.
- Assess history of unexplained syncope, nocturnal agonal respiration, and sudden cardiac arrest.
- Describe the role of specialist assessment, family history, and selected genetic testing.
- Recognize the importance of fever management and avoidance of drugs known to increase risk.
- Understand that ECG findings must be interpreted alongside clinical context.

## 10.6 Arrhythmogenic Cardiomyopathy
- Explain the concept of arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM).
- Describe how myocardial structural and electrical abnormalities may predispose to ventricular arrhythmias.
- Recognize the association between ACM and ventricular ectopy or ventricular tachycardia.
- Identify relevant ECG findings, including selected repolarization and depolarization abnormalities.
- Explain the role of echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging.
- Recognize that disease may affect the right ventricle, left ventricle, or both.
- Describe the contribution of family history and genetic evaluation.
- Differentiate suspected ACM from alternative causes of ventricular arrhythmias.
- Explain how endurance exercise may influence disease expression and arrhythmic risk in susceptible individuals.
- Recognize the importance of individualized risk assessment and specialist follow-up.
- Understand that no single ECG or imaging finding establishes every ACM diagnosis.

## 10.7 Electrolyte Disturbances and Electrophysiology
- Explain how potassium, magnesium, calcium, and other electrolytes influence cardiac electrical activity.
- Recognize hypokalemia as a potential cause of ventricular ectopy and increased arrhythmia susceptibility.
- Explain how hyperkalemia can alter conduction, ECG morphology, and cardiac excitability.
- Recognize the ECG manifestations of severe potassium abnormalities.
- Explain how hypomagnesemia may contribute to ventricular arrhythmias and torsades de pointes.
- Recognize that hypocalcemia may prolong the QT interval, primarily through ST-segment prolongation.
- Explain how hypercalcemia may shorten the QT interval.
- Differentiate electrolyte-related ECG changes from other causes of conduction and repolarization abnormalities.
- Assess the relevance of renal dysfunction, medications, and acute illness.
- Recognize that severe electrolyte abnormalities may require urgent clinical assessment and treatment.
- Correlate ECG findings with laboratory results and the patient's clinical condition.
- Follow established protocols for electrolyte correction and rhythm monitoring.

## 10.8 Multidisciplinary Communication of EP Findings
- Explain the importance of clear communication between electrophysiologists and the wider clinical team.
- Summarize arrhythmia diagnoses, relevant ECG findings, and electrophysiological mechanisms.
- Differentiate confirmed findings from suspected diagnoses and unresolved questions.
- Communicate clinically significant rhythm abnormalities promptly.
- Present relevant findings in a concise and logically organized format.
- Explain the significance of EP study results for treatment planning.
- Communicate device interrogation findings and documented arrhythmia episodes.
- Coordinate with cardiology, emergency medicine, anesthesia, cardiac surgery, and other teams when appropriate.
- Adapt technical explanations to the recipient's clinical role and level of expertise.
- Use structured handovers to communicate risks, interventions, and follow-up needs.
- Protect patient confidentiality and follow approved communication procedures.
- Document important discussions, recommendations, and agreed clinical plans.

## 10.9 EP Procedure and Finding Documentation
- Document the indication, relevant history, and planned EP procedure.
- Record baseline rhythm, conduction intervals, and relevant intracardiac measurements.
- Describe pacing protocols, stimulation sites, and significant electrophysiological responses.
- Document induced arrhythmias and the evidence supporting the proposed mechanism.
- Record mapping findings, ablation targets, and procedural endpoints when applicable.
- Distinguish observed findings from mechanistic interpretation.
- Document complications, interventions, and the patient's response.
- Record device settings or relevant interrogation findings when applicable.
- Identify limitations, incomplete assessments, and unresolved diagnostic questions.
- Ensure documentation is accurate, objective, timely, and internally consistent.
- Follow institutional standards for procedure reports and electronic health records.
- Correct documentation errors through approved amendment procedures.
- Protect patient information and comply with applicable confidentiality requirements.

## 10.10 Evidence-Based EP Practice
- Explain the role of evidence-based medicine in electrophysiology.
- Identify current professional society guidelines relevant to arrhythmia diagnosis and treatment.
- Differentiate clinical guidelines, consensus statements, systematic reviews, and individual research studies.
- Assess study design, population characteristics, endpoints, and limitations.
- Interpret relative risk, absolute risk, confidence intervals, and statistical significance.
- Recognize the difference between statistical significance and clinical importance.
- Evaluate evidence concerning medications, catheter ablation, device therapy, and monitoring strategies.
- Identify when guideline recommendations may not directly apply to an individual patient.
- Explain the role of shared decision-making and patient preferences in clinical practice.
- Recognize emerging technologies and assess their evidence base before adoption.
- Update clinical knowledge as guidelines and high-quality research evolve.
- Communicate uncertainty and avoid overstating the strength of available evidence.
- Apply institutional policies and professional standards alongside current evidence.

## 10.11 Clinical Reasoning and Technical Quality
- Integrate ECG interpretation, ambulatory monitoring, device data, imaging, and clinical history.
- Recognize discrepancies between symptoms and recorded rhythm findings.
- Identify potential technical artifacts before assigning an arrhythmia diagnosis.
- Assess the reliability and limitations of automated algorithms.
- Recognize when additional monitoring or specialist investigation may be needed.
- Differentiate a diagnostic observation from a proposed underlying mechanism.
- Reassess conclusions when new clinical information becomes available.
- Use a structured approach to differential diagnosis and risk assessment.
- Recognize limitations in personal expertise and seek appropriate consultation.
- Maintain professional competence through continuing education and supervised practice.

## 10.12 Competency and Knowledge Assessment
- Interpret Holter, event monitor, and implantable loop recorder findings.
- Recognize exercise-related arrhythmias and associated warning signs.
- Explain the clinical features of long QT syndrome and Brugada syndrome.
- Describe the electrophysiological features of arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy.
- Identify ECG changes associated with potassium, magnesium, and calcium disturbances.
- Communicate complex EP findings accurately to multidisciplinary teams.
- Document procedures and electrophysiological findings comprehensively.
- Apply evidence-based guidelines and critically evaluate clinical research.
- Recognize diagnostic uncertainty and escalate high-risk findings appropriately.
- Work within professional scope, training requirements, and institutional protocols.

## Expected Learning Outcome
Demonstrate advanced foundational competence in ambulatory rhythm analysis, exercise-related arrhythmias, inherited arrhythmia syndromes, arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, electrolyte-related electrophysiological changes, multidisciplinary communication, accurate procedural documentation, and evidence-based EP practice. Integrate technical findings with clinical context, recognize diagnostic limitations, and support safe, evidence-informed patient care.
