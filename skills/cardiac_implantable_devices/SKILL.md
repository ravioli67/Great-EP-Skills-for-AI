# 7. Cardiac Implantable Electronic Devices

## 7.1 Fundamentals of Cardiac Implantable Electronic Devices
- Explain the purpose of cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs).
- Differentiate permanent pacemakers, implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs), and cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) devices.
- Identify common device components, including pulse generators, leads, electrodes, and connectors.
- Explain the roles of sensing, pacing, detection algorithms, and stored diagnostic data.
- Differentiate single-chamber, dual-chamber, and biventricular systems.
- Describe transvenous and selected leadless pacing systems.
- Explain the importance of device identification, manufacturer information, and implant records.
- Recognize how underlying cardiac disease influences device selection.
- Understand the roles of device specialists and electrophysiologists in device management.
- Follow professional scope, training requirements, and institutional protocols.

## 7.2 Permanent Pacemaker Indications
- Explain the purpose of permanent pacing in clinically significant bradyarrhythmias.
- Identify common indications involving symptomatic sinus node dysfunction.
- Recognize advanced and complete atrioventricular (AV) block as potential pacing indications.
- Differentiate Mobitz type I from Mobitz type II AV block in clinical context.
- Explain pacing considerations in selected patients with conduction disease and syncope.
- Recognize the importance of symptom-rhythm correlation.
- Identify potentially reversible causes of bradycardia before permanent device decisions.
- Explain how medications, ischemia, metabolic abnormalities, and transient conditions influence assessment.
- Describe the role of ventricular function and anticipated pacing burden in device selection.
- Recognize that pacing indications depend on clinical circumstances and current guideline recommendations.
- Avoid recommending implantation from an isolated ECG finding without appropriate clinical evaluation.

## 7.3 Pacemaker ECG Interpretation
- Identify pacemaker spikes and assess their relationship to atrial and ventricular activity.
- Recognize atrial-paced, ventricular-paced, and dual-chamber-paced rhythms.
- Identify paced QRS morphology and assess ventricular activation patterns.
- Recognize atrial pacing followed by native AV conduction.
- Differentiate ventricular pacing from intrinsic ventricular conduction when possible.
- Explain how pacing mode influences the appearance of a paced ECG.
- Recognize fusion and pseudofusion beats.
- Assess pacing spikes, capture, and subsequent depolarization.
- Recognize that some modern devices may produce pacing outputs that are difficult to see on surface ECGs.
- Correlate surface ECG findings with device interrogation when needed.

## 7.4 Pacemaker Sensing and Capture
- Define sensing as the device's detection of intrinsic cardiac electrical activity.
- Define capture as successful myocardial depolarization following a pacing stimulus.
- Differentiate atrial sensing, ventricular sensing, and pacing output.
- Explain the relationship between pacing output and myocardial capture threshold.
- Recognize undersensing and oversensing.
- Explain how sensing settings influence pacing behavior.
- Identify the consequences of failure to capture.
- Recognize how lead displacement, fracture, insulation defects, and tissue changes may affect capture.
- Explain the importance of confirming capture on ECG and device recordings.
- Differentiate electrical capture from mechanical contraction and effective circulation.
- Recognize that surface ECG interpretation alone may not fully characterize device function.

## 7.5 Pacemaker Threshold Assessment
- Define the pacing capture threshold.
- Explain how pacing thresholds are assessed during device interrogation.
- Describe the relationship between pulse amplitude, pulse duration, and capture.
- Explain the purpose of programming an appropriate safety margin above the measured threshold.
- Recognize changes in thresholds associated with lead maturation, medications, ischemia, and electrolyte abnormalities.
- Identify possible causes of an unexpectedly elevated capture threshold.
- Explain how lead integrity and electrode-tissue interface affect pacing performance.
- Recognize differences between atrial and ventricular capture thresholds.
- Explain why threshold measurements must be interpreted alongside sensing and impedance data.
- Understand that threshold testing and programming require appropriate device expertise and clinical protocols.

## 7.6 Pacemaker Programming Modes
- Explain the standardized nomenclature used to describe pacing modes.
- Differentiate single-chamber from dual-chamber pacing.
- Explain the basic functions represented by the first three letters of common pacing mode codes.
- Describe demand pacing and inhibition by sensed intrinsic activity.
- Explain the purpose of rate-responsive pacing.
- Recognize common modes, including AAI, VVI, DDD, and selected rate-responsive variants.
- Explain AV delay and its role in coordinating atrial and ventricular activation.
- Describe mode switching in response to detected atrial tachyarrhythmias.
- Explain how programming can reduce unnecessary ventricular pacing when appropriate.
- Recognize pacemaker syndrome and the effects of AV dyssynchrony.
- Understand that programming must be individualized to the indication, rhythm, and device system.

## 7.7 Pacemaker Malfunction
- Recognize failure to pace, failure to capture, and failure to sense.
- Differentiate undersensing from oversensing using ECG and device information.
- Identify pacing inhibition caused by inappropriate sensing.
- Recognize inappropriate pacing caused by sensing or programming abnormalities.
- Explain how lead fracture, insulation failure, displacement, or connector problems may affect device function.
- Assess changes in lead impedance, sensing amplitude, and capture threshold.
- Recognize possible battery depletion or generator malfunction.
- Differentiate true malfunction from expected device behavior, fusion, and pseudofusion.
- Identify potential electromagnetic interference and myopotential-related sensing abnormalities.
- Recognize symptoms that may indicate clinically significant device dysfunction.
- Explain the need for urgent evaluation when pacing failure occurs in a pacing-dependent patient.
- Correlate ECG findings with device interrogation, imaging, and clinical assessment.

## 7.8 Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillator Indications
- Explain the purpose of an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD).
- Differentiate primary prevention from secondary prevention of sudden cardiac death.
- Recognize ventricular fibrillation and selected sustained ventricular tachycardia as potential secondary-prevention indications.
- Explain how left ventricular function and underlying cardiac disease influence primary-prevention assessment.
- Describe the role of reversible causes and recovery after acute cardiac events.
- Recognize the importance of optimized medical therapy and appropriate waiting periods when applicable.
- Explain how inherited arrhythmia syndromes may influence ICD consideration.
- Differentiate ICDs from pacemakers in their primary therapeutic functions.
- Describe the importance of shared decision-making, comorbidities, prognosis, and patient preferences.
- Apply current guidelines and individualized specialist assessment when evaluating ICD eligibility.

## 7.9 ICD Electrograms and Stored Arrhythmia Episodes
- Explain how ICDs record intracardiac electrograms and detected arrhythmia episodes.
- Identify common near-field and far-field electrogram channels.
- Assess ventricular rate, rhythm regularity, and electrogram morphology.
- Differentiate ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation detections from supraventricular tachyarrhythmias.
- Recognize oversensing, noise, and lead-related signals that may mimic arrhythmias.
- Explain how detection zones, duration criteria, and discrimination algorithms affect device classification.
- Review episode onset, duration, termination, and delivered therapies.
- Correlate stored electrograms with symptoms and available surface ECGs.
- Recognize that device labels are not always equivalent to a definitive clinical diagnosis.
- Document the interpretation of stored episodes and identify uncertain findings.

## 7.10 Antitachycardia Pacing and ICD Shock Therapies
- Explain antitachycardia pacing (ATP) as a treatment for selected tachyarrhythmias.
- Describe how pacing sequences may interrupt reentrant ventricular tachycardia.
- Differentiate ATP from conventional bradycardia pacing.
- Explain the purpose of ICD cardioversion and defibrillation therapies.
- Recognize appropriate and inappropriate ICD therapies.
- Explain common causes of inappropriate therapy, including supraventricular arrhythmias and oversensing.
- Describe how detection zones and programmed therapy sequences influence treatment delivery.
- Recognize the clinical significance of repeated ICD shocks or electrical storm.
- Explain the need for urgent assessment after multiple shocks or associated symptoms.
- Recognize that ICD therapy delivery does not by itself establish the precise underlying rhythm mechanism.
- Understand that therapy programming requires individualized specialist assessment.

## 7.11 Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy
- Define cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT).
- Differentiate CRT with pacing alone (CRT-P) from CRT with defibrillation capability (CRT-D).
- Explain how ventricular electrical dyssynchrony can impair cardiac mechanical performance.
- Recognize the role of QRS duration, QRS morphology, ventricular function, symptoms, and underlying disease in CRT assessment.
- Explain the importance of left ventricular lead placement and effective biventricular pacing.
- Describe the intended coordination of right and left ventricular activation.
- Recognize factors associated with reduced CRT response, including inadequate pacing percentage and suboptimal lead position.
- Explain the role of ECGs, echocardiography, and device interrogation in assessing response.
- Recognize phrenic nerve stimulation, lead displacement, and capture failure as potential complications.
- Differentiate biventricular CRT from conduction system pacing approaches.
- Explain why CRT selection and programming require individualized assessment.

## 7.12 Device Follow-Up and Safety
- Explain the purpose of routine in-person and remote device monitoring.
- Review battery status, lead impedance, sensing, thresholds, and pacing percentages.
- Recognize alerts involving lead integrity, battery depletion, and arrhythmia detection.
- Explain the importance of reviewing stored episodes and delivered therapies.
- Recognize symptoms requiring urgent device assessment.
- Describe considerations for device management during surgery, MRI, and exposure to electromagnetic interference.
- Explain the importance of manufacturer-specific instructions and appropriate perioperative planning.
- Recognize the need for individualized device management during end-of-life care.
- Document device findings, clinical interpretation, and follow-up recommendations accurately.

## 7.13 Competency and Knowledge Assessment
- Explain common permanent pacemaker indications.
- Interpret pacemaker ECGs and identify sensing and capture abnormalities.
- Describe threshold assessment and pacing mode programming.
- Recognize common pacemaker malfunctions.
- Explain ICD indications and interpret stored electrograms.
- Differentiate ATP, cardioversion, and defibrillation therapies.
- Explain CRT principles and common causes of suboptimal response.
- Recognize device-related emergencies and escalation requirements.
- Interpret device findings within clinical context and current guidelines.
- Work within professional scope, training requirements, and institutional protocols.

## Expected Learning Outcome
Demonstrate foundational understanding of pacemaker indications, ECG interpretation, sensing and capture, threshold assessment, programming modes, device malfunction, ICD therapies, stored electrogram interpretation, and cardiac resynchronization therapy. Apply these concepts safely and recognize when specialist device assessment is required.
