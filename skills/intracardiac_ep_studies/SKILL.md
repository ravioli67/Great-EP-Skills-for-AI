# 3. Intracardiac Electrophysiology Studies

## 3.1 Preparing Patients for Invasive EP Studies
- Explain the purpose and general workflow of an electrophysiology (EP) study.
- Review the patient's history, symptoms, prior ECGs, cardiac imaging, and relevant investigations.
- Verify patient identity, procedure documentation, consent, and planned procedure.
- Review medications, allergies, anticoagulation status, renal function, and relevant laboratory results.
- Assess fasting requirements and sedation or anesthesia plans according to institutional protocols.
- Identify relevant comorbidities and potential procedural risks.
- Explain procedural monitoring, vascular access, catheter placement, and post-procedure observation.
- Confirm appropriate monitoring, emergency equipment, and resuscitation readiness.
- Provide patient education and address questions before the procedure.

## 3.2 Indications and Contraindications for EP Studies
- Describe common indications, including evaluation of selected tachyarrhythmias, unexplained syncope, conduction abnormalities, and suspected accessory pathways.
- Explain the role of EP studies in defining arrhythmia mechanisms and guiding catheter ablation.
- Recognize situations in which invasive testing may alter diagnosis or management.
- Identify patient-specific factors that may increase procedural risk.
- Assess active infection, vascular access limitations, bleeding risk, pregnancy considerations, and relevant comorbidities.
- Explain that contraindications and procedural timing depend on the clinical indication and individual risk-benefit assessment.
- Recognize the need to stabilize acute medical conditions before elective procedures when appropriate.
- Confirm procedural suitability with the responsible electrophysiologist and clinical team.

## 3.3 Sterile Procedural Technique
- Explain the principles of aseptic technique during invasive EP procedures.
- Describe appropriate hand hygiene, sterile gloves, gowns, masks, drapes, and skin antisepsis.
- Explain the importance of maintaining a sterile field during catheter and equipment handling.
- Recognize potential sources of contamination during vascular access and catheter manipulation.
- Describe sterile preparation of access sites according to institutional protocols.
- Explain infection-prevention measures for catheters, sheaths, connectors, and procedural equipment.
- Recognize breaches in sterile technique and understand the need for immediate corrective action.
- Follow institutional infection-control policies and the supervising team's instructions.

## 3.4 Femoral Venous Access
- Explain the purpose of femoral venous access during an EP study.
- Identify relevant femoral vascular anatomy and the relationship between the vein, artery, and adjacent structures.
- Describe the principles of ultrasound-guided venous access.
- Explain the roles of sterile preparation, local anesthesia, needle visualization, and guidewire confirmation.
- Recognize the importance of confirming venous access before advancing a guidewire or sheath.
- Identify potential complications, including arterial puncture, bleeding, hematoma, vascular injury, and infection.
- Describe methods for recognizing and escalating suspected access-site complications.
- Understand that vascular access and sheath insertion require appropriate training, credentialing, supervision, and adherence to institutional protocols.

## 3.5 Intracardiac Catheter Positioning
- Explain the purpose and basic design of diagnostic EP catheters.
- Identify common catheter recording locations, including the high right atrium, His-bundle region, coronary sinus, and right ventricle.
- Explain how fluoroscopy and electrogram recordings support catheter localization.
- Describe the role of electroanatomic mapping and other imaging methods when used.
- Recognize the importance of stable catheter contact and appropriate electrode positioning.
- Identify potential complications related to catheter manipulation, including arrhythmia induction, perforation, and vascular injury.
- Explain how catheter position affects electrogram quality and interpretation.
- Follow the supervising electrophysiologist's instructions and institutional safety procedures.

## 3.6 Recording Intracardiac Electrograms
- Explain how intracardiac electrodes record local cardiac electrical activity.
- Differentiate intracardiac electrograms from surface ECG recordings.
- Identify the effects of electrode spacing, catheter orientation, filtering, gain, and recording settings.
- Recognize atrial, His-bundle, and ventricular electrogram components.
- Explain the importance of timing relationships between intracardiac signals and the surface ECG.
- Recognize noise, far-field signals, poor contact, and other recording artifacts.
- Explain how multiple recording sites help determine activation sequence and conduction timing.
- Document electrogram findings accurately and avoid conclusions unsupported by the recorded signals.

## 3.7 Identifying Atrial, His-Bundle, and Ventricular Electrograms
- Identify atrial (A), His-bundle (H), and ventricular (V) electrograms on intracardiac recordings.
- Differentiate local atrial activation from far-field ventricular signals.
- Recognize the typical A-H-V sequence during normal antegrade conduction.
- Explain how catheter location influences electrogram morphology and timing.
- Identify His potentials and distinguish them from nearby atrial or ventricular signals.
- Recognize changes in activation sequence during bundle branch block, AV block, and accessory-pathway conduction.
- Correlate intracardiac electrograms with the surface ECG.
- Recognize recording artifacts and avoid misidentifying electrical signals.

## 3.8 Measuring AH and HV Intervals
- Define the AH interval as the time from the atrial electrogram to the His-bundle electrogram.
- Define the HV interval as the time from the His-bundle electrogram to the earliest ventricular activation.
- Measure AH and HV intervals using clearly identifiable intracardiac signals.
- Understand that AH primarily reflects AV nodal conduction, while HV reflects conduction through the His-Purkinje system.
- Recognize that AH duration varies with heart rate, autonomic tone, medications, and pacing conditions.
- Identify prolonged or shortened intervals and interpret them in clinical context.
- Explain how abnormal AH and HV intervals can support evaluation of conduction disease.
- Recognize technical limitations and avoid interpreting intervals without reliable signal identification.

## 3.9 Programmed Electrical Stimulation
- Explain the purpose of programmed electrical stimulation during an EP study.
- Differentiate drive pacing, single extrastimuli, multiple extrastimuli, and burst pacing.
- Explain the S1-S2 pacing protocol and the role of progressively earlier extrastimuli.
- Describe how programmed stimulation can assess conduction, refractoriness, and arrhythmia inducibility.
- Explain the role of stimulation from different cardiac sites.
- Recognize how pacing cycle length and autonomic conditions influence results.
- Describe the limitations of induced arrhythmias when correlating findings with spontaneous clinical events.
- Explain why stimulation protocols must be selected and performed by appropriately trained EP personnel.

## 3.10 Conduction Intervals and Refractory Periods
- Assess atrial, AV nodal, His-Purkinje, and ventricular conduction using intracardiac recordings.
- Define the effective refractory period and functional refractory period.
- Explain how refractory periods are measured using programmed stimulation.
- Identify the AV nodal and accessory-pathway refractory periods when clinically indicated.
- Explain decremental conduction and rate-dependent conduction delay.
- Recognize conduction block and distinguish it from failure to capture.
- Describe how premature stimulation can reveal dual AV nodal physiology and accessory-pathway conduction.
- Explain the relationship between conduction velocity, refractory periods, and reentrant arrhythmias.
- Interpret refractory-period findings in the context of the patient's rhythm, medications, and procedural conditions.
- Document measurements, pacing protocols, findings, and diagnostic limitations accurately.

## Expected Learning Outcome
Demonstrate foundational understanding of intracardiac electrogram identification, AH and HV interval measurement, programmed electrical stimulation, and conduction and refractory-period assessment. Interpret findings in clinical context and recognize the importance of procedural supervision, recording quality, and patient safety.
