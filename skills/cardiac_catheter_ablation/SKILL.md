# 5. Cardiac Catheter Ablation

## 5.1 Core Principles of Catheter Ablation
- Explain the purpose of catheter ablation in treating cardiac arrhythmias.
- Differentiate diagnostic EP studies from therapeutic ablation procedures.
- Identify the arrhythmia mechanism and target substrate before ablation.
- Explain how ablation interrupts abnormal impulse formation or conduction.
- Differentiate focal ablation from linear lesions and substrate modification.
- Describe the roles of electroanatomic mapping, intracardiac electrograms, and imaging.
- Explain the importance of procedural planning, patient selection, and informed consent.
- Recognize that ablation strategy depends on arrhythmia mechanism, anatomy, and clinical risk.
- Understand the roles of the electrophysiologist and supporting clinical team.
- Follow professional scope, institutional protocols, and procedural supervision requirements.

## 5.2 Radiofrequency Ablation Principles
- Explain how radiofrequency (RF) energy generates resistive heating in cardiac tissue.
- Differentiate resistive heating near the electrode from conductive heating in surrounding tissue.
- Describe how temperature, power, duration, irrigation, and tissue characteristics influence lesion formation.
- Explain the difference between temperature-controlled and power-controlled delivery.
- Describe the relationship between lesion depth, lesion width, and energy delivery.
- Explain how electrode size, orientation, and contact influence energy transfer.
- Recognize steam pops, coagulum formation, char, and excessive tissue heating as potential complications.
- Explain the purpose of irrigated-tip ablation catheters.
- Recognize the risks of thrombus formation, collateral tissue injury, and perforation.
- Describe the importance of monitoring system parameters and following the operator's protocol.

## 5.3 Cryoablation Principles
- Explain how cryothermal energy produces tissue injury through cooling and freezing.
- Describe the effects of cooling on cellular structures and tissue viability.
- Differentiate reversible cryomapping effects from permanent cryoablation lesions.
- Explain how freezing and thawing contribute to lesion formation.
- Describe the importance of catheter stability and tissue contact during cryoablation.
- Explain the potential advantages of cryoablation near selected vulnerable structures.
- Recognize the limitations of cryoablation, including lesion size and procedural effectiveness.
- Describe potential complications, including vascular injury, thromboembolism, and collateral tissue damage.
- Compare RF and cryothermal energy according to target anatomy and procedural goals.

## 5.4 Cardiac Mapping and Ablation Systems
- Explain the purpose of electroanatomic mapping systems.
- Differentiate activation mapping, voltage mapping, and pace mapping.
- Explain how three-dimensional cardiac geometry is reconstructed.
- Identify the roles of mapping catheters, reference electrodes, and intracardiac signals.
- Describe how local activation times are measured relative to a reference signal.
- Explain how voltage maps help identify scar and low-voltage tissue.
- Describe the role of contact-force and lesion-quality indicators when available.
- Explain how fluoroscopy, intracardiac echocardiography, and other imaging modalities support navigation.
- Recognize the limitations of mapping accuracy, registration, and catheter localization.
- Operate mapping and ablation interfaces only within training, professional scope, and institutional authorization.

## 5.5 Catheter-Tissue Contact and Lesion Formation
- Explain how catheter contact influences energy delivery and lesion formation.
- Describe the effects of contact force, stability, orientation, and tissue thickness.
- Explain the relationship between energy delivery and lesion transmurality.
- Recognize the importance of lesion contiguity and appropriate lesion spacing.
- Explain how inadequate contact may produce ineffective lesions.
- Describe how excessive contact or energy delivery can increase complication risk.
- Explain the importance of tissue temperature, impedance changes, and available lesion-quality metrics.
- Recognize that surrogate indicators do not independently guarantee lesion durability.
- Explain lesion maturation and the potential for recovery of conduction after ablation.
- Understand the principles of reassessment and confirmation of the intended electrophysiologic endpoint.

## 5.6 Pulmonary Vein Isolation for Atrial Fibrillation
- Explain the role of pulmonary vein triggers in initiating atrial fibrillation (AF).
- Describe the purpose of pulmonary vein isolation (PVI).
- Identify the anatomical relationship between the pulmonary veins and left atrium.
- Explain the principles of circumferential lesion formation around pulmonary vein antra.
- Differentiate wide antral isolation from ablation close to individual vein ostia.
- Describe the roles of electroanatomic mapping, intracardiac echocardiography, and imaging.
- Explain entrance block and exit block as methods of assessing electrical isolation.
- Recognize pulmonary vein reconnection as a potential cause of arrhythmia recurrence.
- Describe complications, including cardiac tamponade, thromboembolism, pulmonary vein stenosis, and collateral injury.
- Explain the importance of esophageal protection strategies and appropriate periprocedural anticoagulation.
- Recognize that additional ablation beyond PVI depends on the clinical indication and documented arrhythmia substrate.

## 5.7 Cavotricuspid Isthmus Ablation for Typical Atrial Flutter
- Explain the macroreentrant mechanism of typical atrial flutter.
- Identify the cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) as a critical component of the typical flutter circuit.
- Describe the anatomical boundaries relevant to CTI-dependent flutter.
- Explain the purpose of creating a continuous line of conduction block across the CTI.
- Describe activation mapping and pacing methods used to assess the flutter circuit.
- Explain bidirectional CTI block as the principal procedural endpoint.
- Recognize the importance of confirming block rather than relying solely on flutter termination.
- Describe possible reasons for incomplete block or later conduction recovery.
- Recognize potential complications, including vascular injury, perforation, and thromboembolism.
- Differentiate typical CTI-dependent flutter from atypical macroreentrant atrial tachycardia.

## 5.8 Slow-Pathway Modification for AVNRT
- Explain the role of dual AV nodal physiology in AV nodal reentrant tachycardia (AVNRT).
- Describe the purpose of slow-pathway modification.
- Identify the general anatomical region targeted while recognizing individual anatomical variation.
- Explain the role of intracardiac electrograms and electroanatomic landmarks.
- Describe junctional rhythm during ablation and its interpretation in procedural context.
- Explain why preservation of normal AV conduction is essential.
- Recognize the risk of AV block and possible pacemaker requirement.
- Describe the role of post-ablation stimulation in assessing tachycardia inducibility.
- Explain why procedural endpoints must be interpreted alongside baseline conduction and clinical findings.
- Recognize recurrence and the need for individualized follow-up.

## 5.9 Accessory Pathway Ablation for AVRT
- Explain how accessory pathways enable atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia (AVRT).
- Differentiate manifest from concealed accessory pathways.
- Describe the role of activation mapping and pathway localization.
- Explain how intracardiac electrograms identify pathway-associated activation.
- Describe the principles of RF or cryothermal ablation according to pathway location and clinical considerations.
- Explain the importance of confirming loss of pathway conduction.
- Recognize the need to assess antegrade and retrograde conduction when applicable.
- Describe the potential for pathway recurrence after initially successful ablation.
- Recognize risks involving AV conduction, coronary structures, and nearby anatomy.
- Explain why left-sided, septal, and epicardial pathways may require different procedural strategies.

## 5.10 Focal Atrial Tachycardia Ablation
- Explain how focal atrial tachycardia may arise from automaticity, triggered activity, or localized reentry.
- Describe the purpose of activation mapping to identify the earliest atrial activation site.
- Explain the use of local electrogram timing relative to the surface P wave.
- Describe pace mapping and its limitations in localizing a focal source.
- Recognize the importance of distinguishing focal activity from macroreentrant atrial tachycardia.
- Explain how catheter stability and accurate anatomical localization influence ablation effectiveness.
- Describe the role of tachycardia termination and subsequent testing in assessing procedural success.
- Recognize the risks associated with ablation near the AV node, sinus node, or other critical structures.
- Explain why recurrence may result from incomplete treatment or an alternative focus.

## 5.11 Ventricular Tachycardia Substrate Ablation
- Explain the mechanisms of ventricular tachycardia (VT), including scar-related reentry.
- Differentiate activation mapping, pace mapping, and substrate mapping.
- Explain how voltage mapping identifies scar and abnormal ventricular tissue.
- Describe the roles of late potentials, fractionated electrograms, and conducting channels in selected substrates.
- Explain the relationship between scar architecture and reentrant VT circuits.
- Differentiate ablation during tolerated VT from substrate-based ablation performed during sinus rhythm or paced rhythm.
- Describe the role of entrainment and activation mapping when VT is sufficiently stable for assessment.
- Explain how ablation targets critical conducting tissue within an arrhythmia substrate.
- Recognize challenges posed by hemodynamic instability, intramural scar, and epicardial substrates.
- Describe complications, including perforation, tamponade, thromboembolism, and injury to the conduction system.
- Explain the importance of assessing procedural endpoints and monitoring for recurrence.
- Recognize that VT ablation strategy depends on the underlying cardiac disease and individual risk.

## 5.12 Procedural Endpoints and Confirmation
- Define acute procedural success in relation to the intended arrhythmia target.
- Differentiate termination of an arrhythmia from elimination of its sustaining mechanism.
- Explain the importance of testing for bidirectional block, electrical isolation, or pathway conduction loss when applicable.
- Describe the role of programmed stimulation and repeat conduction assessment.
- Recognize that noninducibility does not guarantee long-term freedom from arrhythmia.
- Explain how lesion recovery and reconnection can lead to recurrence.
- Document procedural findings and remaining uncertainty accurately.

## 5.13 Complications and Patient Safety
- Recognize vascular injury, bleeding, hematoma, infection, and thromboembolism.
- Explain the recognition and clinical significance of cardiac perforation and tamponade.
- Describe the risks of AV block, sinus node injury, and other conduction disturbances.
- Recognize collateral injury risks involving the esophagus, coronary arteries, phrenic nerve, and adjacent structures.
- Explain the importance of appropriate anticoagulation and thromboembolic risk management.
- Recognize signs of hemodynamic deterioration and escalate immediately.
- Follow radiation safety, sterile technique, and institutional emergency protocols.
- Understand that suspected serious complications require prompt assessment by the responsible clinical team.

## 5.14 Competency and Knowledge Assessment
- Explain RF and cryoablation mechanisms.
- Describe the principles of cardiac mapping and lesion formation.
- Explain catheter-tissue contact and factors influencing lesion durability.
- Describe the goals and endpoints of PVI, CTI ablation, and slow-pathway modification.
- Explain accessory pathway, focal atrial tachycardia, and VT substrate ablation.
- Recognize major procedural complications and safety considerations.
- Interpret procedural endpoints without overstating their implications.
- Distinguish established findings from assumptions and uncertainty.
- Follow professional scope, training requirements, and institutional protocols.

## Expected Learning Outcome
Demonstrate foundational understanding of cardiac catheter ablation energy sources, mapping systems, catheter-tissue interaction, major supraventricular and ventricular ablation procedures, procedural endpoints, and complication prevention. Apply these concepts within professional scope and under appropriate clinical supervision.
