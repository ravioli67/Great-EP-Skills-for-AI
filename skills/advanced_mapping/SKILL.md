# 6. Advanced Mapping and Complex Arrhythmias

## 6.1 Three-Dimensional Electroanatomical Mapping
- Explain the principles of three-dimensional electroanatomical mapping (3D EAM).
- Describe how mapping systems reconstruct cardiac chamber geometry.
- Identify the roles of mapping catheters, reference electrodes, and localization technologies.
- Differentiate magnetic, impedance-based, and hybrid catheter localization systems.
- Explain how intracardiac electrograms are associated with anatomical locations.
- Describe the importance of reference stability and accurate anatomical registration.
- Recognize the effects of catheter movement, respiration, and cardiac motion on mapping accuracy.
- Explain how electroanatomical maps integrate activation timing, voltage amplitude, and anatomical information.
- Identify the limitations of interpolation and incomplete chamber sampling.
- Describe how intracardiac echocardiography, CT, or MRI integration may support anatomical assessment.
- Recognize mapping artifacts and distinguish them from genuine anatomical or electrophysiological findings.
- Interpret mapping results alongside surface ECGs and intracardiac recordings.
- Operate mapping systems within professional scope and appropriate clinical supervision.

## 6.2 Activation Mapping
- Define activation mapping and explain its role in localizing arrhythmia origins and conduction pathways.
- Explain the selection of a stable timing reference for activation measurements.
- Identify local activation times using intracardiac electrogram characteristics.
- Interpret color-coded activation maps and isochronal distributions.
- Recognize the earliest recorded activation site during focal tachycardia mapping.
- Explain how activation sequences help distinguish focal arrhythmias from macroreentry.
- Identify continuous activation patterns that may support a reentrant circuit.
- Recognize that the earliest mapped site may not always represent the true arrhythmia origin.
- Explain how poor catheter contact and far-field signals can distort activation timing.
- Assess whether the map adequately covers the suspected circuit or focus.
- Recognize limitations caused by unstable rhythms, changing cycle lengths, and incomplete mapping.
- Correlate activation maps with pacing maneuvers, surface ECG morphology, and clinical arrhythmia characteristics.

## 6.3 Voltage Mapping
- Define voltage mapping and explain its use in characterizing cardiac tissue.
- Differentiate bipolar from unipolar electrogram voltage measurements.
- Explain how voltage maps represent local electrogram amplitude.
- Recognize that voltage thresholds depend on chamber, catheter, electrode configuration, rhythm, and mapping conditions.
- Identify regions of relatively preserved voltage and reduced voltage.
- Explain how scar, fibrosis, and diseased myocardium may alter electrogram amplitude.
- Recognize the effects of poor contact, electrode orientation, wall thickness, and wavefront direction.
- Differentiate true low voltage from technical or physiological causes of reduced amplitude.
- Explain the role of consistent acquisition settings when comparing maps.
- Integrate voltage findings with anatomical imaging and local electrogram characteristics.
- Avoid interpreting voltage thresholds as definitive histological proof of scar.

## 6.4 Low-Voltage Scar Regions
- Explain the relationship between myocardial fibrosis, scar, and low-voltage electrograms.
- Identify dense scar, border zones, and relatively preserved myocardial regions when supported by mapping data.
- Explain how surviving myocardial bundles can form conducting channels within scar.
- Recognize fractionated electrograms and late potentials in selected scar substrates.
- Describe how scar architecture may support slow conduction and reentrant arrhythmias.
- Differentiate fixed anatomical scar from transient low-voltage recordings caused by poor contact.
- Explain how prior myocardial infarction, cardiomyopathy, surgery, or ablation may produce scar substrates.
- Recognize that low-voltage regions must be interpreted in anatomical and clinical context.
- Explain how substrate characterization can guide further electrophysiological assessment.
- Document scar distribution, recording limitations, and mapping criteria accurately.

## 6.5 Entrainment Mapping
- Define entrainment and explain its role in evaluating reentrant tachycardias.
- Describe pacing at a cycle length faster than the tachycardia cycle length.
- Explain how entrainment captures and accelerates the tachycardia circuit.
- Differentiate manifest from concealed entrainment.
- Explain the significance of fusion between paced and tachycardia activation patterns.
- Describe the relationship between pacing site, conduction delay, and post-pacing interval.
- Compare the post-pacing interval with the tachycardia cycle length when measurements are reliable.
- Explain how entrainment may help identify critical components of a reentrant circuit.
- Recognize the importance of confirming capture and stable tachycardia before interpreting responses.
- Identify limitations caused by decremental conduction, pacing-site distance, and circuit instability.
- Avoid assuming that entrainment alone proves a specific arrhythmia mechanism.
- Integrate entrainment findings with activation maps, electrograms, and other diagnostic maneuvers.

## 6.6 Pace Mapping
- Define pace mapping and explain its role in arrhythmia localization.
- Describe how paced QRS morphology is compared with the morphology of a clinical ventricular arrhythmia.
- Explain the importance of consistent lead placement and recording conditions.
- Identify how pacing site and myocardial conduction influence QRS morphology.
- Describe the use of pace mapping in selected ventricular arrhythmias and focal arrhythmia assessments.
- Recognize limitations caused by preferential conduction, deep myocardial sources, and intramural substrates.
- Differentiate a similar paced QRS morphology from proof of the exact arrhythmia origin.
- Explain how pace mapping can complement activation mapping and substrate mapping.
- Recognize that pace mapping may be less informative when the arrhythmia origin is deep or poorly accessible.
- Document pacing locations, morphology comparisons, and interpretation limitations.

## 6.7 Accessory Pathway Localization
- Explain the anatomical and electrophysiological basis of accessory atrioventricular pathways.
- Differentiate manifest, intermittent, and concealed accessory pathways.
- Recognize surface ECG evidence of ventricular pre-excitation.
- Explain how intracardiac activation timing helps localize pathway conduction.
- Describe the role of annular mapping along the atrioventricular junction.
- Identify the use of atrial and ventricular pacing in assessing pathway conduction.
- Explain how earliest local activation may help identify pathway-associated sites.
- Recognize that pathway location affects mapping strategy and procedural risk.
- Describe the general anatomical distinctions between left-sided, right-sided, septal, and less common pathway locations.
- Explain why para-Hisian and septal pathways require particular attention to normal conduction-system structures.
- Recognize that multiple pathways or unusual conduction patterns may complicate localization.
- Confirm pathway participation using integrated electrophysiological evidence rather than surface ECG morphology alone.

## 6.8 Complex Atrial Tachycardia Circuits
- Differentiate focal atrial tachycardia from macroreentrant atrial tachycardia.
- Explain how surgical scars, prior ablation lesions, and structural heart disease may create reentrant pathways.
- Describe common mechanisms of atypical atrial flutter and scar-related atrial tachycardia.
- Explain the use of activation mapping to reconstruct atrial activation sequences.
- Identify continuous activation patterns that may support macroreentry.
- Recognize conduction gaps and narrow channels between anatomical barriers.
- Explain how entrainment can help assess whether a mapped region participates in the tachycardia circuit.
- Describe the role of high-density mapping in complex atrial substrates.
- Recognize how incomplete chamber coverage may produce misleading circuit interpretations.
- Differentiate a true critical isthmus from a bystander region.
- Explain the importance of identifying an appropriate electrophysiological endpoint.
- Recognize the risks of creating additional atrial scar and potentially promoting new arrhythmias.
- Integrate mapping findings with prior procedural history and documented clinical rhythms.

## 6.9 Scar-Related Ventricular Tachycardia Circuits
- Explain the role of myocardial scar in reentrant ventricular tachycardia (VT).
- Describe how surviving myocardial channels can support slow conduction within scar.
- Differentiate activation mapping during tolerated VT from substrate mapping during sinus or paced rhythm.
- Identify late potentials, local abnormal ventricular activities, and fractionated electrograms when present.
- Explain the role of entrainment in assessing participation of a mapped site in a stable VT circuit.
- Describe how pace mapping can support localization of potential VT exit regions.
- Recognize the difference between a circuit's critical isthmus and a bystander pathway.
- Explain how scar border zones and conducting channels may guide substrate assessment.
- Recognize that hemodynamic instability can limit activation and entrainment mapping.
- Describe how endocardial mapping may fail to identify an intramural or epicardial substrate.
- Explain the role of imaging and clinical history in understanding scar distribution.
- Recognize the risks of ventricular perforation, tamponade, conduction injury, and thromboembolism during invasive procedures.
- Assess procedural findings using defined endpoints without assuming that all potential circuits have been eliminated.

## 6.10 Potential Epicardial Arrhythmia Substrates
- Explain the distinction between endocardial, intramural, and epicardial arrhythmia substrates.
- Identify clinical and imaging findings that may suggest an epicardial substrate.
- Recognize selected cardiomyopathies and scar patterns associated with epicardial ventricular arrhythmias.
- Explain how standard endocardial mapping may provide incomplete information about epicardial disease.
- Describe the role of surface ECG morphology and imaging in generating substrate hypotheses.
- Recognize that ECG morphology alone cannot reliably confirm an epicardial origin.
- Explain the general role of pericardial access and epicardial mapping in selected specialist procedures.
- Identify potential complications, including coronary artery injury, phrenic nerve injury, bleeding, and pericardial complications.
- Explain why epicardial procedures require specialized expertise, imaging, and procedural planning.
- Recognize that epicardial ablation decisions depend on clinical indication, anatomical risk, and available evidence.
- Interpret suspected epicardial involvement alongside endocardial mapping and other diagnostic findings.

## 6.11 Mapping Integration and Diagnostic Limitations
- Integrate activation, voltage, pace, and entrainment mapping when appropriate.
- Correlate three-dimensional maps with intracardiac electrograms and surface ECG findings.
- Recognize inconsistencies between mapping modalities and investigate possible technical causes.
- Assess whether data density and anatomical coverage are sufficient for the intended interpretation.
- Distinguish observed electrical findings from inferred anatomical mechanisms.
- Recognize that map quality depends on catheter contact, reference stability, signal filtering, and rhythm consistency.
- Document mapping parameters, anatomical assumptions, limitations, and key findings.
- Avoid making definitive claims when the available mapping data are incomplete or ambiguous.

## 6.12 Competency and Knowledge Assessment
- Explain the principles and limitations of three-dimensional electroanatomical mapping.
- Interpret activation and voltage maps.
- Identify potential low-voltage scar regions and conducting channels.
- Explain entrainment and pace mapping.
- Describe methods for accessory pathway localization.
- Differentiate focal and macroreentrant atrial tachycardia mechanisms.
- Explain scar-related ventricular tachycardia circuits.
- Recognize findings that may suggest an epicardial arrhythmia substrate.
- Integrate multiple sources of electrophysiological evidence.
- Work within professional scope, training requirements, and institutional protocols.

## Expected Learning Outcome
Demonstrate foundational understanding of advanced electroanatomical mapping, activation and voltage interpretation, scar characterization, entrainment and pace mapping, accessory pathway localization, complex atrial and ventricular arrhythmia circuits, and potential epicardial substrates. Interpret findings critically, recognize technical limitations, and apply appropriate procedural safety standards.
